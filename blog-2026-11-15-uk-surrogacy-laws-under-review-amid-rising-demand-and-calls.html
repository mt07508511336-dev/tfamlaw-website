UK Surrogacy Laws Under Review Amid Rising Demand and Calls for Reform

15 November 2026

Surrogacy has become an increasingly popular route to parenthood in the UK, with applications for parental orders nearly doubling over the past decade. This surge has ignited discussions about the adequacy of current surrogacy laws and the need for reform to better support families and surrogates alike.

Under existing UK legislation, surrogacy arrangements are permitted on an altruistic basis, meaning surrogates can only receive reasonable expenses, not profit. The woman who gives birth is legally recognized as the mother until a parental order is granted, transferring parenthood to the intended parents. This process can be lengthy and complex, often leaving families in legal limbo during a crucial time.

In response to these challenges, the Law Commission has proposed significant reforms. Key recommendations include recognizing intended parents as the legal parents from birth, provided certain pre-conception checks and agreements are in place. This change aims to streamline the legal process and provide clarity and security for all parties involved.

However, these proposals have sparked debate. Advocates argue that such reforms would simplify the surrogacy process, encourage domestic arrangements, and offer better protections for surrogates and intended parents. Critics, on the other hand, express concerns that immediate recognition of intended parents could undermine a surrogate's right to change her mind, especially in cases where she has a genetic connection to the child. There's also apprehension that easing domestic surrogacy procedures might inadvertently increase demand for international surrogacy, potentially leading to the exploitation of women in countries with less stringent regulations.

Despite the Law Commission's recommendations, the government has indicated that surrogacy law reform is not currently a legislative priority. This stance leaves many families navigating a complex and outdated legal framework, underscoring the pressing need for a balanced approach that safeguards the interests of surrogates, intended parents, and, most importantly, the children born through these arrangements.
